\section*{Sources}

This note compares the HexOpt objective in two sources.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]\sloppy
  \item \textbf{Paper.} H.~Tong and Y.~J.~Zhang, ``Fast and Robust Hexahedral Mesh Optimization via Augmented Lagrangian, L-BFGS, and Line Search,'' \href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.11656}{arXiv:2410.11656}, Equations~(2.5), (2.6), (2.8), and (2.9).
  \item \textbf{\texttt{conspire}.} Branch \texttt{msj} at commit \texttt{515c78cee}, draft \href{https://github.com/mrbuche/conspire.rs/pull/251}{mrbuche/conspire.rs\#251}, file \texttt{src/\allowbreak geometry/\allowbreak mesh/\allowbreak quality/\allowbreak improve/\allowbreak msj/\allowbreak mod.rs}, functions \texttt{evaluate} and \texttt{terms}.
\end{itemize}

$h$ is one hex element of the mesh $H$, and $\ell(h)$ is its average edge length.
$J(h)$ is the minimum of the Jacobian $\det(\mathbf{e}_0, \mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2)$ over nine points of $h$: its eight corners and its body center (paper, Section~2.1).
At a corner, $\mathbf{e}_0$, $\mathbf{e}_1$, and $\mathbf{e}_2$ are the three edge vectors that meet there.
At the center, they join the centers of opposite faces.
$\Jh(h)$ is the minimum of the scaled Jacobian $\det(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_0, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2)$ over the same nine points, where $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_k = \mathbf{e}_k / |\mathbf{e}_k|$.
The two minimums can occur at different points, so $\Jh(h)$ is in general not $J(h)$ divided by a product of lengths.
\texttt{conspire} computes both minimums in \texttt{terms}, each over all nine points.
$\Theta \in [0, 1]$ is the threshold for the minimum scaled Jacobian $\Jh$.
Both sources hold $\ell$ fixed when they take the gradient.

This note's notation differs from the paper's in four symbols.
Subscripts $\mathrm{p}$ and $\mathrm{c}$ mark the paper's and \texttt{conspire}'s forms of $\psi$.

\section*{Definitions}

Both sources use the same term for an inverted hex:
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\mathrm{p}}(h) = \psi_{\mathrm{c}}(h) = J(h) / \ell, \qquad J(h) \le 0.
\end{equation}
For $J(h) > 0$, the paper defines
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\mathrm{p}}(h) =
  \begin{cases}
    \ell^2 \, \Jh(h), & \Jh(h) \le \Theta, \\
    \Theta, & \Jh(h) > \Theta,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and \texttt{conspire} implements
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\mathrm{c}}(h) = \ell^2 \min\bigl(\Jh(h), \Theta\bigr).
\end{equation}
The two differ only above $\Theta$, where $\psi_{\mathrm{p}} = \Theta$ and $\psi_{\mathrm{c}} = \Theta \, \ell^2$.
$\psi_{\mathrm{c}}$ is one expression: $\ell^2$ times $\Jh$ clamped at $\Theta$.
$\psi_{\mathrm{p}}$ has no such form unless $\ell = 1$.
Figure~\ref{fig:psi} plots both forms.

\section*{Units}

$J$ has units of length$^3$, since each $\mathbf{e}_k$ has units of length.
$\Jh$ and $\Theta$ have no units.

  \midrule

  $J > 0$, $\Jh \le \Theta$ & length$^2$ & length$^2$ \\
  $\Jh > \Theta$ & \textbf{none} & length$^2$ \\

\section*{The minimized function}

At each threshold $\Theta$, \texttt{conspire} minimizes the augmented Lagrangian
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{h \in H} \psi_{\mathrm{c}}(h) + \sum_{i} \Bigl[ \lambda_i \, r_i + \frac{\rho}{2} \, r_i^2 \Bigr]. \label{eq:L}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The first sum has one term per hex, with $\psi_{\mathrm{c}}$ as defined above.
  \item The second sum holds the boundary on the target STL, with a multiplier term and a quadratic penalty.
    $r_i = (\mathbf{n}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}) - d_i$ is the signed distance from a boundary node $\mathbf{x}$ to the plane of one STL triangle.
    $\mathbf{n}_i$ is the triangle's unit normal, and $d_i = \mathbf{n}_i \cdot \mathbf{y}$ for any vertex $\mathbf{y}$ of that triangle.
    $\lambda_i$ is the Lagrange multiplier for plane $i$, and $\rho$ is the penalty coefficient.
    Each boundary node gets up to four planes: the plane of its closest triangle, plus the planes of nearby triangles whose normals differ from those already kept by more than $30^\circ$.
  \item After each L-BFGS run, \texttt{conspire} updates each multiplier, $\lambda_i \leftarrow \lambda_i + \rho \, r_i$.
    $\rho$ starts at 100 and doubles each time the mesh meets $\Theta$ with the boundary on the STL.
  \item $r_i$ and $\lambda_i$ have units of length, and $\rho$ has none.
    Both constraint terms have units of length$^2$.
\end{itemize}
The paper's Equation~(2.8) has the same structure.
Its constraint is $\mathbf{Z}_k - \mathbf{Z}^t_k$, the offset from each boundary vertex to its closest point on the target surface.
Its Equation~(2.9) updates the multipliers the same way, and it doubles $\rho$ each time the minimum scaled Jacobian reaches $\Theta$.
The paper's $L$ uses $\psi_{\mathrm{p}}$ in place of $\psi_{\mathrm{c}}$. The next section returns to that difference.

\section*{Behavior at the threshold}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Continuity.}
    At $\Jh = \Theta$, both forms below $\Theta$ give $\Theta \, \ell^2$.
    Above $\Theta$, $\psi_{\mathrm{c}}$ gives the same value, so it is continuous at $\Theta$.
    Above $\Theta$, $\psi_{\mathrm{p}}$ gives $\Theta$, so it jumps by $\Theta (\ell^2 - 1)$ unless $\ell = 1$.
    Neither form is differentiable at $\Theta$.
    The slope drops to zero there in both, by design.

  \item \textbf{The sign of the jump.}
    $\mathcal{L}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:L} sums $-\psi$ over all hexes.
    Under $\psi_{\mathrm{p}}$, a hex that rises above $\Theta$ changes $\mathcal{L}$ by $+\Theta (\ell^2 - 1)$.
    For $\ell > 1$, $\mathcal{L}$ rises.
    The Armijo condition requires $\mathcal{L}$ to fall, so the jump counts against every step that lifts such a hex above $\Theta$.
    For $\ell < 1$, $\mathcal{L}$ falls, so the jump rewards the crossing instead.
    The sign depends on the length unit of the mesh.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item On the snapped bone octree in the reference frame, $\ell$ runs from 0.644 to 2.81, with a median of 1.253.
        The same mesh has hexes on both sides of $\ell = 1$.
      \item On the scale-5 bone mesh in the STL's own units, $\ell$ runs from 0.009 to 0.034.
    \end{itemize}
    Under $\psi_{\mathrm{c}}$, the crossing changes $\mathcal{L}$ by zero.

  \item \textbf{Units.}
    $\psi_{\mathrm{c}}$ has units of length$^2$ in every case.
    \texttt{conspire}'s types enforce this: each term is a \texttt{Quantity<Area>}.
\end{enumerate}

For a hex that stays above $\Theta$ through a step, both forms are constants.
They give the same gradient and the same energy differences.
The forms differ only when a hex crosses $\Theta$.
